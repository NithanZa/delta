# Ground-truth audit and trust-budget research for Delta

## Executive findings

Delta’s competitive-intelligence work is directionally strong, but several of its most consequential errors are exactly the sort that can damage trust in a research product: it sometimes collapses billing cadence into a “price cut,” treats roadmap items as shipped features, and turns incomplete public evidence into categorical claims. The most important corrections are that Buffer’s apparent 2024 move from $6/$12/$120 to $5/$10/$100 was primarily the annual-billing equivalent, not a clean across-the-board monthly price cut; Buffer’s guest-access feature was still explicitly “coming soon” on June 17, 2026; Buffer’s apparent $10-vs-$12 Team pricing discrepancy is annual versus monthly billing, not stale documentation; and SocialPilot plainly does have a visible affiliate program. citeturn453154search3turn919109search3turn453154search2turn529198search3

The larger strategic readings are mostly sound. Buffer did not abandon agencies when its dedicated Agency pricing tier disappeared: by 2026 it had a dedicated agency page, agency case studies, agency-oriented Team-plan material, volume pricing, and a formal Agency Partner Program. SocialPilot likewise remains strongly agency-oriented while deliberately stretching downward toward independents/SMBs and upward toward multi-location brands and enterprise requirements such as SSO, API access, advanced security, and dedicated account management. citeturn453154search0turn198433search3turn529198search5turn529198search6

For trust, there is **no defensible universal empirical threshold at which “X% errors makes users abandon an AI research product.”** Existing research instead gives a more useful pattern: users begin with substantial skepticism; citations increase trust even when the citations are bad; users who actually inspect citations become less trusting when things do not check out; and monitoring-product users consistently complain about irrelevant alerts, duplicates and stale content. That argues for an asymmetric trust budget: near-zero tolerance for fabricated quotes, very low tolerance for hard factual errors, considerably more tolerance for explicitly probabilistic hypotheses, and no penalty at all for a truthful “nothing material changed” digest. citeturn665186academia6turn665186search1turn665186search2turn524025search0

For evaluation, Delta’s existing historical-cutoff setup is unusually promising. It closely resembles point-in-time research benchmarks such as FinanceGym, where retrieval is restricted to documents available before a cutoff and performance is judged separately on what was knowable then versus what happened later. The right harness is therefore not one aggregate “accuracy” number, but separate gates for quote fidelity, atomic factual precision, citation entailment/completeness, temporal leakage, change-detection precision/recall, duplicate/stale rate, and confidence calibration. citeturn976644search2turn976644search0turn976644search1turn976644academia6

## Part B — Trust budget

There is no published research that supports a clean statement such as “users abandon research products after 3% factual error.” Trust is conditional on task stakes, user expertise, error visibility and the kind of claim being wrong. The evidence does, however, strongly support treating **provenance failures as more damaging than ordinary uncertainty**. Li and Aral’s large-scale experiment—about 12,000 search queries and roughly 80,000 results—found that reference links and citations increased trust even when they were incorrect or hallucinated. That means citations confer a credibility premium that the system must not abuse. citeturn665186academia6

Ding et al.’s AAAI experiment found the same basic asymmetry: the mere presence of citations increased reported trust even for random citations, while users who actually checked citations reported less trust. The study does **not** establish a universal numerical “one false citation reduces trust by X% for the rest of the report,” so such a figure should not be invented. It does establish why a fabricated or mismatched citation is qualitatively dangerous: the system has made a strong verifiability promise, and the user discovers that the promise itself is unreliable. citeturn665186search1turn665186search7

General public trust starts from a low base. Pew’s August 2025 representative U.S. survey found that among people who had encountered AI search summaries, 53% had at least some trust, but only 6% trusted them “a lot,” while 46% had little or no trust. Gartner separately reported in 2025 that 53% of surveyed consumers distrusted or lacked confidence in AI-powered search and summaries. These are not CI-user churn studies, but they show that a research assistant cannot assume a large reservoir of pre-existing trust. citeturn665186search2turn665186search5

Citation-grounded products have responded by making source inspection part of the product rather than hiding it. OpenAI Deep Research says reports include citations/source links, a sources-used section and activity history so users can verify the work. Perplexity added a “Check Sources” capability and a side panel showing the passage behind an individual citation. Gemini exposes source panels and explicitly warns users that sources can still be incomplete or wrong. These are all product-design signals that provenance inspection is becoming table stakes for serious research workflows. citeturn987971search4turn987971search9turn987971search6turn987971search8

Competitive-intelligence products exhibit a different trust failure: **too much low-value information**. A 2026 analysis of 500 verified G2 reviews across Klue, Crayon, Kompyte and Contify found that irrelevant alerts were the only negative theme occurring at a material rate across all four products; Klue reviewers also specifically complained about syndicated duplicate stories cluttering the feed. This is secondary research rather than vendor telemetry, but it is unusually on-point for Delta’s product class. citeturn524025search0turn524025search1

The monitoring literature points in the same direction. A 2026 systematic review of alert-fatigue research found recurring use of alert quantity, acceptance/override behavior, clinical relevance and false-alert measures; the central phenomenon is reduced attention or response when alerts are excessive or inappropriate. Health-care alerting is obviously a higher-stakes and different domain from competitive intelligence, so its exact rates should **not** be transferred to Delta, but the mechanism—false positives consuming scarce attention until users start ignoring the feed—is directly relevant. citeturn524025search4

### Recommended weekly trust budget

These thresholds are **product-quality recommendations**, not empirically discovered universal abandonment points. They intentionally set a much higher standard than the current average performance reported for generative-search systems. For context, a 2023 human audit of four generative search engines found only 51.5% of generated sentences fully supported by citations and only 74.5% of citations supporting their associated sentence; a 2026 study of commercial LLMs/deep-research agents found 3–13% of citation URLs appeared hallucinated and 5–18% did not resolve. Those rates are benchmarks of the problem, not acceptable targets for a paid/relied-on CI system. citeturn665186search3turn524025search2

| Error class | Recommended target | Release / trust-budget boundary | Why |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| **Wrong hard facts** | **≤1% of atomic factual claims** on an 8-week rolling sample | **Block a digest/release above 2%**, or on **any material error** in pricing, availability, security, acquisition/ownership or strategic conclusion | Users can tolerate occasional low-impact mistakes, but a verified wrong price or feature entitlement is highly salient. Factual precision should therefore be much closer to 99% than to current generative-search baselines. citeturn665186search3turn665186search2 |
| **Quotes not actually on the cited page** | **0% target** | **Any fabricated or materially altered quote blocks release** | A verbatim quote is Delta’s strongest provenance claim. Citation experiments show citations create extra trust; when users check and find that evidence does not match, trust falls. There is no good reason to spend error budget here because quote matching can be mechanically tested. citeturn665186search1turn665186academia6 |
| **Stale or duplicate digest items** | **≤5% of surfaced items** | Investigate at **>5%**; block/rerank at **>10%** | Noise is the only material complaint shared across four major CI platforms in the 500-review study; G2 users specifically call out syndicated duplicate stories. The product’s scarce resource is user attention. citeturn524025search0turn524025search1 |
| **Hypotheses later shown wrong** | Measure by confidence, not one flat rate. For **≥0.80 confidence**, later contradiction should stay **≤10–15%**; for **0.60–0.79**, **≤25–30%** is reasonable | Block new model/prompt if rolling **Brier score >0.20**, calibration error is clearly worsening, or high-confidence misses exceed **15%** once the sample is large enough | A hypothesis explicitly labeled uncertain is not a factual hallucination. What matters is whether “80%” claims come true about 80% of the time. Proper probabilistic scoring and reliability diagrams are designed for exactly this distinction. citeturn987971search0turn987971search1 |
| **“Nothing new this week” digests** | **No minimum novelty quota**; empty is acceptable whenever true | Keep **false-empty rate ≤5%** on known material changes; **any miss of a major pricing/ownership/security change is severe** | Manufacturing novelty to avoid an empty report creates the same noise problem that CI users already dislike. “Nothing material changed” should become a trust-building result if the system can demonstrate what it checked. citeturn524025search0 |

For Delta specifically, I would make **quotes and temporal leakage zero-budget errors**. A tool whose proposition is “I extracted this exact fact and here are the source words” should not use the same tolerance for an altered quote that it uses for a strategic hypothesis later turning out wrong. The former is an integrity failure; the latter is an expected property of calibrated inference.

## Part C — Evaluation methodology

Citation-grounded research systems need to be evaluated at a finer granularity than whole-answer correctness. ALCE explicitly separates **fluency, correctness and citation quality** and found that even its best systems lacked complete citation support on half of ELI5 examples. AttributionBench asks whether **every claim is fully supported by its cited evidence** and shows that automated attribution judging itself is imperfect: even a fine-tuned GPT-3.5 reached only about 80% macro-F1 on its binary attribution task. That is an important warning against letting an LLM judge be the sole release gate. citeturn976644search0turn976644search1

FActScore provides the right unit of analysis for Delta’s factual layer: break prose into **atomic facts** and compute the fraction supported by reliable evidence. Its core insight is especially applicable here because one Delta “insight” may contain five correct pieces and one wrong commercial detail; a single whole-paragraph pass/fail label hides that failure mode. citeturn976644academia6

Delta’s confidence scores require a different evaluation family. For a binary eventual outcome \(y_i\in\{0,1\}\) and a predicted probability \(p_i\), use the Brier score:

\[
\text{Brier}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(p_i-y_i)^2.
\]

Lower is better, but the score should not be read in isolation because its value also depends on event prevalence and difficulty. Pair it with a **reliability diagram**: among hypotheses assigned roughly 0.8 confidence, approximately 80% should ultimately resolve true. Modern calibration literature treats this matching between predicted probabilities and observed frequencies as the defining idea of calibration. citeturn987971search0turn987971search1turn987971search2

The historical-cutoff design in Delta is already close to a best-practice research-agent backtest. FinanceGym gives an agent a research question and cutoff date, hard-restricts retrieval to documents published on or before that cutoff, and separates grading of what was knowable at the time from what happened afterward. Delta should adopt the same conceptual separation: **ground-truth correctness at cutoff** is one score; **forecast/hypothesis quality after cutoff** is another. Never use later evidence to rescue a weakly grounded historical claim. citeturn976644search2turn976644search3

### Minimal weekly harness for a solo developer

The evaluation record should be generated **before** the prose report. For every digest item, store the competitor, item ID, cutoff timestamp, claim text, claim type (`fact`, `quote`, `change`, `hypothesis`), source URL, source publication/capture date, retrieval timestamp, quote span, confidence, and a stable hash of the source snapshot. That makes the provenance layer testable independently of the LLM’s writing.

For human labeling, sample **25 digest items per week**, but automatically check **all quotes and all source dates**. A sensible stratification is ten claimed changes, five “no material change” or negative findings, five hypothesis updates and five random items from the remaining output. Decompose those items into atomic claims; in practice that should produce a substantially larger number of fact judgments than 25 without forcing a solo developer to manually review the entire feed. The weekly rate estimates will still be noisy, so use an **eight-week rolling window** for percentage gates while retaining immediate blockers for zero-tolerance failures.

Each atomic fact should receive one of five labels: **Supported, Partially supported, Unsupported, Contradicted, or Post-cutoff/Stale**. Separately label source quality as primary/secondary/user-generated and whether the cited passage actually entails the claim. This mirrors the attribution distinction used in ALCE/AttributionBench rather than treating “a URL exists” as proof. citeturn976644search0turn976644search1

The core metrics should be:

| Metric | Definition | Suggested gate |
|---|---|---:|
| **Atomic factual precision** | Supported atomic hard facts ÷ all hard facts reviewed; “partial” should not count as fully correct | **≥98% hard floor; ≥99% target** |
| **Quote fidelity** | Quotes that are literal matches to the archived/source text after harmless whitespace normalization | **100%** |
| **Citation entailment precision** | Cited claims for which the cited passage actually supports the asserted claim | **≥98%** |
| **Citation completeness** | Factual claims requiring evidence that have an adequate citation | **≥95%**, with **100% for pricing/feature/ownership claims** |
| **URL/source validity** | Citation resolves or has a preserved snapshot and is the intended source | **≥99.5%; zero fabricated URLs** |
| **Point-in-time compliance** | Evidence published/captured on or before cutoff | **100%** |
| **Material-change alert precision** | True material changes ÷ surfaced material-change alerts | **≥90%** |
| **Material-change recall** | Known material changes recovered ÷ all seeded/known material changes | **≥95%** on the historical test set |
| **Stale + duplicate rate** | Surfaced items that are stale, repeated or syndicated duplicates | **≤5%** |
| **Brier score for resolved hypotheses** | Mean squared probability error on resolved forecasts | **≤0.20** as an initial operating gate, interpreted against baseline |
| **High-confidence contradiction rate** | Resolved hypotheses emitted at ≥0.80 that turn out false | **≤15%**, with a long-run target closer to ≤10% |
| **Calibration error** | Gap between confidence bins and empirical success rate | **≤0.10** once sample size is adequate |
| **False-empty rate** | Weeks marked “nothing material new” despite a known material change | **≤5%; major miss = severe error** |

The **quote checker should be deterministic**, not an LLM judgment. Normalize Unicode and whitespace, then require the quoted words to exist as a contiguous span in the preserved page text. If Delta shortens a quote, require an explicit ellipsis; if it paraphrases, remove quotation marks. A source URL that later disappears should not automatically fail if Delta preserved the retrieved snapshot and can prove the source existed at the cutoff. Rao et al.’s 2026 work is useful here because it demonstrates that URL health and likely fabricated-versus-stale references can be checked automatically at scale. citeturn524025search2

For **change detection**, keep a small “golden timeline” for Buffer, SocialPilot and perhaps three additional competitors. Seed it with known events such as pricing changes, plan removals, acquisitions, API launches, major feature launches and program launches. Each historical test has a cutoff and a set of material changes that become discoverable after it. Run Delta against that frozen corpus. This gives you both precision—did it hallucinate change?—and recall—did it notice what actually changed?—without leaking later pages into the evidence set. FinanceGym’s mandatory cutoff on every retrieval call is a good model: one post-cutoff result should invalidate the run’s point-in-time status rather than merely incur a small accuracy penalty. citeturn976644search2

For **hypotheses**, do not score “strengthened/weakened” as if those were facts. Persist the probability before the future evidence arrives and later assign an outcome of true/false, with a separate `ambiguous/unresolved` status that is excluded until resolvable. Generate the Brier score and reliability diagram over a rolling set of at least roughly 50 resolved hypotheses before drawing strong conclusions from calibration bins; until then, show counts alongside every rate. A confidence value that never gets backtested is decoration rather than calibrated confidence. citeturn987971search1turn987971search0

For **human labeling on a one-person team**, maintain a one-page rubric with examples of the five support labels. The developer should ideally label the evidence **without looking at Delta’s confidence score first**, reducing anchoring. Re-label a small fixed subset from previous weeks to measure self-consistency. When an automated LLM judge disagrees with the human label, route the disagreement to manual review rather than averaging the two. AttributionBench’s roughly 80% macro-F1 result for a strong automatic evaluator is enough evidence that an automated attribution judge should be a triage mechanism, not the final arbiter. citeturn976644search1

For **prompt/model/retriever changes**, maintain a fixed regression suite of roughly **40–50 historical cases** covering the failure modes seen in this audit: annual-vs-monthly price ambiguity, plan removal, conflicting official pages, roadmap-versus-shipped status, absence claims such as “no partner program,” cross-client permission leakage, and source-date cutoff enforcement. Run it on every meaningful prompt, model or retrieval change. A release should be blocked by **any fabricated quote, any post-cutoff evidence, any fabricated URL, any severe pricing/ownership mistake, a fall of more than two percentage points in factual or citation precision versus the current production baseline, or an absolute metric falling below the gates above**.

The most important architectural principle is to keep **facts, quotations, changes and hypotheses as different trust objects**. FActScore-style atomicity is appropriate for facts; exact string verification for quotations; precision/recall and deduplication for change feeds; Brier/reliability analysis for hypotheses; and hard temporal constraints for historical backtests. Rolling all of those into one “Delta accuracy = 92%” number would erase the distinctions that matter most to users. citeturn976644academia6turn976644search0turn987971search1turn976644search2
